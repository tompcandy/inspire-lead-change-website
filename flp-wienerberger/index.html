Read 1 · About 15 minutes

The four values — on your shift

Values are easy to hang on a wall and hard to see on a Tuesday night shift. This programme treats them differently: every value is defined by what your team can watch you do.

Read each value below twice. First as it's written. Then again, asking: when did my team last see me do this? If the honest answer is "not recently", you've just found your starting point — and you're exactly who this programme is for.

TRUST

We act with integrity — fairly, consistently, reliably. We back our people and trust their expertise.

  • You do what you said you'd do by the shift you said you'd do it.
  • When someone raises a problem, your first reaction makes them glad they did.
  • You defend your team's decisions upwards when they acted in good faith.

RESPECT

We know our people and listen. We recognise effort as well as results. We stay humble, embrace differences, and model inclusion every day.

  • You know what each operator is good at, what they're working on, and one thing that matters to them outside work.
  • You notice effort out loud — not just the numbers.
  • You ask before you tell, especially with people who've run the line longer than you.

PASSION

We make goals tangible and translate site targets into language the team owns. We celebrate success — even on the tough shifts.

  • Your team can say what this week's goal is and why it matters, in their own words.
  • When the team hits a target, something visible happens.
  • Your energy on a bad day is deliberate, because the shift catches whatever mood you bring.

CREATIVITY

We encourage decisiveness and dare to dream big. We create space to learn and stay curious. We say no to what isn't important.

  • When something goes wrong, your first question is "what happened?", not "who did it?"
  • You act on ideas from the team — and tell them what happened to their idea.
  • You protect the team's focus by saying no to the things that don't matter.

You'll work with these values on all three workshop days. Day 1 anchors on Trust and Respect, Day 2 on Passion and Creativity, and Day 3 pulls all four together.

Read 2 · About 20 minutes

The 10 behavioural principles, explained

The values are the why. These ten principles are the what — the observable behaviours your 360 measures, the workshops practise, and your 100-day plan will track.

Open each principle. For each one you'll find what it means, what it looks like on the floor, what it isn't — and one question to sit with. Don't rush; this is the spine of the whole programme.

1Respectfully challenge each otherRespect · Trust

What it means: speaking up when something isn't right — a standard slipping, a risk being taken, a decision you disagree with — clearly and without damaging the relationship.

On the floor: "Before we run this batch — the check sheet from nights isn't signed. Let's close that first." Said calmly, to anyone, regardless of rank or friendship.

What it isn't: being harsh, scoring points, or the opposite — staying silent to keep the peace. Silence is also a choice, and it has consequences.

When did you last see something not right and say nothing? What made silence feel safer?

2Listen first to understandRespect

What it means: listening to understand what the other person actually means — not waiting for your turn to speak, and not jumping straight to a fix.

On the floor: a one-to-one where the operator talks more than you do. Asking "what else?" and counting to five before filling a silence.

What it isn't: nodding while planning your answer. Fixing a problem the person didn't ask you to fix. Listening only when there's time — there never is; you make it.

Who on your team do you know least well — and what would a real conversation with them look like?

3Set clear expectationsPassion · Trust

What it means: people know exactly what's expected — behaviour, performance and safety — before they're held to it.

On the floor: "Aisles clear, tools shadowed, checks signed by end of shift" instead of "keep the area tidy". The expectation answers: what, by when, to what standard, and how we'll both know.

What it isn't: standards that exist only in your head. Holding someone to a rule nobody told them is a fairness problem, and teams feel it.

Which of your expectations exists only in your head right now?

4Use data and stick to the factsTrust

What it means: conversations about performance, quality and problems run on evidence, not opinion or volume.

On the floor: "Three of five handovers this week missed the material count" instead of "nights always leave us short". When you don't have the data, you say so — and go and get it.

What it isn't: using data as a weapon to win arguments. Facts are for understanding problems together, not for beating people with charts.

What data do you already have access to that you never use in team conversations?

5Focus on the process, not the personRespect · Trust

What it means: when something goes wrong, the first question is "where did the process let us down?" — not "whose fault is this?"

On the floor: "The torque check was missed on three units — let's find out how" instead of "you're careless". Thanking the person who reports a near-miss, every time.

What it isn't: nobody ever being accountable. It's the opposite: accountability goes up when blame goes down, because problems surface while they're still small.

Think of the last blame moment on your shift. What did it teach people about reporting the next problem?

6Ensure quality at sourcePassion

What it means: quality is built in at every station, not inspected in at the end. The standard is owned where the work happens.

On the floor: stopping the line when something's on the edge — and backing the person who stops it, even if they turn out to be wrong.

What it isn't: "quality is the quality department's job", or passing a borderline batch downstream because the shift target is close.

What does it currently cost someone on your shift to stop the line? What would make the right call cheaper?

7Be prepared and look aheadCreativity · Passion

What it means: anticipating problems instead of discovering them. Firefighting looks heroic; preparation looks boring and wins.

On the floor: ten minutes before the shift on four questions — people, materials, machines, risks. Removing a blocker before the team hits it. Saying no to a distraction so the team can focus.

What it isn't: pretending everything is predictable. It's about catching the smoke, because almost every fire had some.

Think of your last firefight. When was the earliest moment someone could have seen it coming?

8Provide structured feedback for improvementTrust · Respect

What it means: feedback that is specific, recent, about behaviour, and delivered as a conversation — both corrective and positive.

On the floor: "In this morning's handover, the downtime issue from nights wasn't mentioned, so the day crew lost an hour. What happened?" — situation, behaviour, impact, then a question.

What it isn't: vague praise ("good job"), stored-up criticism delivered once a year, or the feedback sandwich — the bread hides the filling.

How much specific positive feedback did your people get from you last week — and would they agree?

9Understand the goalPassion

What it means: everyone on the shift knows what the goal is, why it matters, and their part in it — in their own words, not management language.

On the floor: "Fast, clean changeovers are how we win this week" instead of "OEE must reach 82%". Wins are celebrated so people feel them.

What it isn't: reading numbers off a board. A goal your team can't repeat is a goal your team can't hit.

If you asked one of your operators what this week's target is and why it matters — what would they honestly say?

10Create what the customer valuesCreativity · Passion

What it means: connecting daily decisions on the line to the real person who receives the work — the builder, the site, the family whose wall or roof it becomes.

On the floor: "Is this good enough?" becomes a different question when you know who's laying these bricks on Thursday.

What it isn't: "the customer" as an abstract word in a presentation. Customers are specific, and your team deserves to see the end of the chain.

Who actually uses what your shift made yesterday? Could you name them?

Your 360 report — which you'll open at the start of Workshop 1 — rates you on exactly these ten behaviours, as seen by your manager, your peers and your team. The principles you've just read are the language of your whole programme.

Read 3 · About 15 minutes

Psychological safety on the shopfloor

Why do people sit on bad news? Not because they don't care. Because of what happened to the last person who spoke up.

In the late 1990s, Harvard researcher Amy Edmondson set out to prove that the best hospital teams made the fewest medication errors. The data said the opposite: the best teams appeared to make more errors. It took her months to see why. The best teams weren't making more mistakes — they were reporting more, because it was safe to. The weaker teams were hiding theirs.

She called this psychological safety: the shared belief that you won't be punished or humiliated for speaking up with questions, concerns, mistakes or ideas. Thirty years of research since — in hospitals, aircraft crews, factories and mines — points the same way: teams that feel safe to speak up have fewer accidents, catch quality problems earlier, and improve faster. Not because they're softer. Because they see their problems sooner.

What this looks like on a shift

Psychological safety is not politeness, and it is not lowering the bar. A shift can be direct, demanding and completely safe — that's the combination this programme is after. The test is simple: what happens to the person who brings bad news?

An operator notices a bearing running hot. Reporting it means paperwork, maybe being told they're overreacting, maybe a dig from a colleague about slowing the line. Saying nothing costs them nothing — tonight. Multiply that quiet calculation across every operator, every shift, every week, and you have the real reason problems "come out of nowhere". They never do. Somebody always saw the smoke.

"The standard you walk past is the standard you accept — and the silence you create is the silence you'll get."

The theme of Workshop 1

The leader is the thermostat

Here is the uncomfortable part: on a shift, psychological safety is set almost entirely by the leader — one reaction at a time. When someone raises a near-miss and your first response is irritation, the whole team recalibrates. When someone stops the line in good faith and gets thanked — even if they were wrong — the whole team recalibrates the other way.

Three behaviours build it, and you'll practise all three on Day 1:

React well to bad news. First response: "thank you for telling me". Evaluate later. Your first ten seconds decide whether you hear the next one.

Blame the process, not the person (Principle 5). "What happened?" opens doors that "who did this?" closes permanently.

Admit your own mistakes out loud. Nothing makes it safer to be fallible than a leader who visibly is. "I called that wrong yesterday — here's what I'm changing" costs you nothing and buys you the truth.

Before Workshop 1

Watch your own shift for one week with this lens. Notice one moment where someone spoke up — what made it possible? And notice one moment where someone probably didn't — what made silence the safer bet? Bring both observations with you. We'll use them.

Your worksheet and your 360

Two things to complete before Workshop 1. Both come with you on the day.

The pre-work worksheet

One page of "Why I lead" reflection, plus three real workplace situations you want to work through — each tagged to the principle you think is most at play. This is the raw material for your practice sessions on Day 1.

Your pre-work checklist

  1. Complete your 360. You'll receive the link by email — it takes your raters about 15 minutes, so nominate them early. Your confidential report is sealed until Workshop 1.
  2. Read the three pieces on this page. Values, the 10 principles, psychological safety. 50–60 minutes all together.
  3. Fill in the worksheet. "Why I lead" plus your three situations, tagged to principles. Print it or write it — either is fine, as long as it comes with you.
